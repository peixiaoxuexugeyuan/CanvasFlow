# Prompt Blueprint

Use this blueprint to keep the final prompt complete without forcing identical stories onto different paintings.

## Opening paragraph

State:

- artwork or visible scene used as reference;
- duration and aspect treatment;
- living realistic scene first, painting at the end;
- true one-take and fast expressive camera intent;
- emotional center and dominant environmental force.

## Time-coded movement

For a default 30-second piece, use four or five continuous phases. Each phase must start where the previous phase physically ends.

### Phase A: fast entry

- Begin with a strong spatial position already suggested by the painting.
- Start camera movement immediately.
- Establish foreground blur, midground destination, stable background, and the dominant environmental force.
- Do not begin inside an object unless the image genuinely supports an open, physically traversable space.

### Phase B: discover and pursue

- Reveal the secondary subject or anchor through continuous movement.
- Give the subject an emotionally readable action.
- Keep the subject continuously visible when overtaking, passing, or shifting sides.
- Maintain a stable horizon or background cue so the viewer understands the route.

### Phase C: main subject and camera peak

- Approach the principal subject with the strongest continuous curve.
- Use a broad orbit, parallel track, rise, descent, or overtaking move.
- Reach the speed or emotional peak here.
- Slow briefly only for the key look, touch, light, gesture, or relationship.

### Phase D: spatial release and alignment

- Continue from the same curve into a retreat, widening arc, crane, or descent.
- Reveal the full composition quickly and clearly.
- Move subjects into the original painting's arrangement through their own continuous actions, not morphing or teleportation.

### Phase E: materialization and hold

- Stop the camera only after composition alignment.
- Reduce motion in layers.
- Convert reality into the painting through material, edge, palette, and brushwork changes.
- Hold the finished painting long enough to read.

## One-line camera route

After the time-coded section, restate the full route in one line, for example:

`low uphill track -> parallel pursuit -> visible overtaking -> broad outside arc -> continuous backward descent -> original low-angle composition`

This sentence is a continuity audit. If it cannot be written clearly, redesign the route.

## Foreground blur language

Good:

- flowers and grass skim the lower and side edges;
- the central sightline stays open;
- near details blur directionally while the subject remains sharp;
- foreground density increases speed perception without hiding spatial continuity.

Avoid:

- fills the entire frame;
- completely covers the lens;
- darkness or blur conceals the transition;
- emerges on the other side in a new location;
- passes through an umbrella, body, wall, or flower.

## Speed language

Prefer concrete movement over the adjective "fast":

- accelerates uphill while maintaining a stable horizon;
- overtakes the subject along a visible outside curve;
- performs a broad half-orbit with believable inertia;
- releases the confined foreground into a wide landscape;
- brakes progressively over the final four seconds.

Include selective motion blur, parallax, changing apparent scale, and audio perspective. Do not use random whip-pans or repeated speed ramps.

## Painterly conversion

Describe conversion in scene-specific order. Usually move from environment to large props, clothing, skin, and fine details, or follow the direction of light or wind. Preserve positions and silhouettes throughout.

Name only the palette and brush behavior visible in the reference. Avoid generic claims about an artist's whole career.

## Sound section

Include:

- close sounds tied to near-lens motion;
- subject sounds tied to visible action;
- distant ambience;
- continuous changes in direction and distance;
- a final natural fade.

End with an explicit prohibition on music and musical sound design.

## Stability section

List only relevant risks. For example:

- a parasol remains rigid and keeps the same canopy, ribs, and handle;
- two children remain distinct people with consistent clothing and faces;
- lantern flame stays contained and does not become a fire effect;
- wind direction remains coherent;
- the final camera angle and figure placement match the reference.
